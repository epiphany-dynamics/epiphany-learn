**No, but the job changes. AI already drafts, summarizes, and sorts faster than any human, so document review, chronology building, and first-pass research shift first. Privilege calls, factual verification, client context, and accountable filing stay with a person who signs their name to the work.**

If you work in a law office and you have watched a colleague paste a discovery batch into a chat window and get a clean summary back in ten seconds, the worry is reasonable. This article separates the parts of the job that are genuinely moving from the parts that are not, and gives you something safe to practice.

## What replacing a paralegal would actually require

**Replacement is not one event. A tool would have to absorb every task on your desk, including the ones nobody wrote down. AI is strong at producing text from documents you give it. It is weak at knowing which documents exist, which are privileged, and which are quietly wrong.**

Think about a normal week. You chase a client for a signature. You notice that an exhibit referenced in a motion was never actually produced. You remember that this particular judge wants courtesy copies. You calendar a deadline correctly because you know the triggering event, not just the rule.

A language model does none of that. It responds to what is in front of it. It has no independent view of the file, no memory of the last three matters with this client, and no professional exposure if the filing is wrong. That last point matters more than people expect. Courts have sanctioned lawyers for filings containing citations that a chatbot invented. The accountability did not transfer to the software. It stayed with the humans who filed.

So the honest framing is not "replaced" or "safe." It is: which tasks are moving, and what is left when they go.

## The task split: what moves first, what stays

**Sort your work by whether a mistake is visible on the page or only visible to someone who knows the matter. Errors a careful reader can catch move to AI first with review. Errors that require knowing the client, the file, or the rule stay human for now.**

| Task | What AI handles reasonably now | What still needs you |
|---|---|---|
| First-pass document review | Sorting, tagging, plain summaries of what a document says | Deciding what is responsive, relevant, or worth flagging |
| Chronology building | Pulling dates and events into a timeline draft | Confirming every entry against the source and catching gaps |
| Legal research first draft | Explaining a concept, suggesting search directions | Verifying every case exists, is good law, and says what is claimed |
| Cite checking | Formatting, spotting inconsistent citation style | Confirming the pin cite actually supports the proposition |
| Deposition summaries | A readable first pass from a clean transcript | Knowing which three answers the attorney will care about |
| Privilege review | Nothing you should trust unsupervised | All of it. Privilege is a judgment call with real consequences |
| Client communication | Drafting a clearer version of your words | The relationship, the tone, and knowing what not to say |
| Filing and deadlines | Reminders and checklist drafts | The calendar entry, the trigger date, and the accountability |

The pattern is the same one showing up in other document-heavy roles. The same split appears in the honest task breakdown for whether AI replaces project managers, where status reports automate and stakeholder judgment does not, and in the task breakdown on whether AI replaces recruiters, where screening moves faster than the decision about a person. Legal work has one extra layer: a duty of competence and confidentiality that does not bend for convenience.

## A safe exercise you can run this week

**Build a chronology from a matter you invent. Write ten fake emails about a fictional contract dispute, save them as one file, and ask an assistant to produce a dated timeline with a source line for every entry. Then check every entry yourself against the file.**

Nothing real goes in. That is the point. You are testing the tool, not doing work.

1. **Invent the matter.** Ten short emails between two made-up companies about a late delivery. Include two conflicting dates on purpose, and one email with no date at all.
2. **Save them as a single document.** A plain text or Word file is fine.
3. **Ask for a specific output.** Try: "From the attached emails only, build a table with three columns: date, event, and the exact quoted sentence you took it from. If a date is unclear or missing, write UNCLEAR rather than guessing."
4. **Check every row.** Open your file and find each quoted sentence. Mark each row correct, wrong, or invented.
5. **Look at the two traps.** Did it flag the conflicting dates or silently pick one? Did it write UNCLEAR for the undated email, or invent a date from context?
6. **Write down your error rate.** Not to publish. So you know, from your own testing, how much checking this tool needs.

Most people are surprised twice. The output looks better than expected, and the checking takes longer than expected. Both are true at once, and both are useful to know before anyone asks you to use this on a live matter.

## Where this goes wrong, honestly

**The failure mode is not a tool that refuses to answer. It is a tool that answers confidently with a date that does not appear anywhere in your file. Confidentiality is the second risk. Client documents in a consumer chat account may sit outside what your firm agreed to protect.**

On the first risk, it helps to understand the mechanism rather than treating it as a glitch. The plain-English explanation of [why ChatGPT gives wrong answers](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) covers why a model produces a plausible citation with the same confidence it produces a real one. There is no internal signal telling it which is which. That is why a source line and your own verification are not optional extras.

On the second, read [is ChatGPT confidential](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) before you upload anything from a real file. Consumer accounts, firm-licensed accounts, and enterprise agreements have different terms, and your firm may have client-specific obligations on top.

**When not to do this at all:** live matters, sealed filings, anything under a protective order, anything involving a client who has not consented, and any workflow your firm has not approved in writing. If you are unsure whether your office has a policy, that question goes to your supervising attorney before your next upload, not after.

## Your adaptation plan for the next ninety days

**Aim at two things: verification skill and matter knowledge. Verification means you can prove any AI output against a source in under a minute. Matter knowledge means you understand why the case is shaped the way it is. Both get harder to automate as the tools get better.**

A simple checklist, no budget required:

- **Weeks 1 to 2.** Run the fake-matter exercise above twice, on different document types. Keep your error notes.
- **Weeks 3 to 4.** Build one reusable prompt for a task you repeat, with a source-line requirement baked in. Test it on invented documents only.
- **Weeks 5 to 8.** Ask your supervising attorney what the firm's policy is and offer to write the one-page checklist if none exists. That single document makes you the person who understands the risk, not the person creating it.
- **Weeks 9 to 12.** Deepen matter knowledge on one active case. Learn the procedural posture, the opposing counsel's pattern, the client's real priority. None of that is in the documents.

The uncertain part, said plainly: nobody knows how fast the routine layer thins out, and anyone giving you a confident date is guessing. What is predictable is direction. Volume tasks compress, and the value moves toward the person who catches the error before it reaches a judge. That is the same conclusion reached in the calm answer to [will AI take my job](/articles/will-ai-take-my-job), and it holds up better than either panic or reassurance.

Start with the fake matter this week. When you can state your own error rate from your own testing, you are already ahead of most of the conversation, and the [plan your first project module](/modules/module-6/first-project) is the natural next step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will AI replace paralegals in the next five years?

The role is unlikely to disappear, but the mix of work is already shifting. Firms are applying AI to document review, summaries, and research drafts first because those are high volume and checkable. Paralegals who become the verification layer stay valuable. Paralegals whose entire day is first-pass summarizing face the most pressure.

### Should I tell my supervising attorney that I use AI?

Yes, before you use it on anything real. Many firms have written policies, some have client-specific restrictions, and a few prohibit it entirely. Raising it proactively positions you as the person managing the risk. Discovering it later, after an error, positions you very differently.

### Is it safe to put case documents into ChatGPT?

Not by default. Terms differ between consumer accounts, firm-licensed accounts, and enterprise agreements, and your duty of confidentiality is separate from any vendor's promise. Practice on invented documents until your firm has approved a specific tool and a specific workflow.

### What single skill is worth learning first?

Fast verification. Being able to take an AI-generated chronology or research summary and confirm every claim against a source, quickly and reliably, is the skill that keeps its value as the drafting tools improve. It is also the one that protects the filing.

### Do smaller firms adopt this more slowly?

Often, though not always. Smaller firms have less procurement overhead and sometimes move faster than large ones with formal review committees. The more useful question is what your specific office has approved, which you can answer this week by asking rather than guessing.
