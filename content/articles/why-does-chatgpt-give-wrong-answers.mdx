**ChatGPT gives wrong answers because it is not looking anything up. It predicts which words plausibly come next, based on patterns in the text it was trained on. Plausible and true overlap most of the time, but not always, and the model has no built-in way to tell the difference before it answers.**

Most people meet this problem the same way. An answer reads perfectly, gets used in a report or an email, and turns out to be wrong in one small, specific spot. Once you understand what the tool is actually doing, that failure stops being mysterious and starts being predictable, which means you can catch it.

## What ChatGPT is actually doing when it answers

**Think of it as a very strong pattern completer. Given your question and everything written so far, it scores which word is likely to come next, then does that again and again. There is no lookup step, no database check, and no moment where it consults a source and reports back.**

Imagine autocomplete on your phone, grown enormous and trained on an enormous amount of writing. It learned which continuations tend to follow which beginnings. Ask it what year a particular hospital opened, and it does not retrieve a record from a file. It produces something year shaped that fits the sentence.

When a fact appeared often and consistently in the text it learned from, the plausible continuation and the true one are the same string, and you get a right answer. When a fact appeared rarely, appeared in contradictory forms, or never appeared at all, the model still produces a plausible continuation. It does not stop and tell you the well ran dry.

### The part that surprises people

There is no internal flag that says "I am guessing here." The system does not maintain a separate record of what it knows versus what it is improvising. So the honest answer to "why did it make that up" is that from the inside, nothing different happened. It ran the same process it always runs.

## Why the confident tone is not a signal

**Fluency and accuracy are produced by the same process, so they do not travel together. The model writes a shaky guess in the same steady voice it uses for a well known fact. Nothing in the wording tells you which one you are reading, which is why tone is a poor safety check.**

With people, we lean on hesitation. A colleague who is unsure slows down, says "I think," or tells you to double check. Those cues are how we allocate our own attention, and they are almost entirely absent here. The smoothness you are reading comes from patterns in how sentences are built, not from anything about whether the claim holds.

This gets worse in exactly the wrong place. Rare, specific details produce the shortest and cleanest sounding answers, because there is no messy debate in the training text to reflect. A crisp, tidy, one line answer to an obscure question deserves more suspicion than a rambling one, not less.

## Where wrong answers cluster

**Errors gather around specifics: names, dates, page numbers, citations, prices, statute numbers, and anything that changed recently. These are details a pattern completer can only approximate, because the shape of a plausible citation is easy to imitate and the exact one is not. Broad explanations hold up far better than precise particulars.**

| Kind of detail | Why it is fragile | Fast check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person paired with a role, quote, or company | Many similar names sit in similar sentences | Search the name and the claim together |
| Dates, version numbers, deadlines | These change, and old text outnumbers new text | Check the official page for that thing |
| Citations, page numbers, case or docket numbers | The format is easy to imitate, the content is not | Search the exact title in quotes |
| Prices, fees, plan limits | Published values move faster than training data | Open the vendor pricing page |
| Menu paths inside a specific app | Interfaces get redesigned constantly | Look at your actual screen |
| Anything recent | It may sit past what the model saw | Ask when the information is from, then verify |

Notice the pattern: the risk is not in the reasoning, it is in the particulars stitched into the reasoning. That is also why a spreadsheet answer can be structurally sensible and still break. ## A safe exercise you can run in five minutes

**Pick a topic you already know cold, then ask about a detail inside it. Your own knowledge becomes the answer key, so you can watch the failure happen without risk. Do this once and the abstract warning turns into something you have seen with your own eyes.**

1. Choose a subject where you are the expert: your former employer, your town, a hobby you have kept for years, a book you have read twice.
2. Ask three broad questions about it. Most answers will be reasonable. This is the part that builds false comfort, so notice how good it feels.
3. Now ask for a specific: an exact founding year, the name of a specific department head, a page reference, a policy number.
4. Grade it yourself. Mark each specific right, wrong, or unverifiable.
5. Ask the same specific again in a fresh chat. If the answer shifts between sessions, you have found a soft spot, because a stored fact does not wobble.

Doing this on ground you know is the whole point. You get the lesson at zero cost, instead of learning it from a client email that already went out.

## The three-question check that costs about a minute

**Before you use an answer, ask three things: which parts are checkable specifics, where would this be published, and what would change if it were wrong. Most answers pass in under a minute. The ones that do not are exactly the ones worth opening a second tab for.**

1. **Which parts are specifics?** Underline the names, numbers, dates, and citations. The prose around them is usually fine. Those underlined bits are your verification list, and often there are only one or two.
2. **Where would this live if it were true?** A real price lives on a pricing page. A real law lives on a government site. If you cannot name a place this fact would be published, that is a warning by itself.
3. **What breaks if this is wrong?** A wrong analogy in a draft costs nothing. A wrong filing deadline costs a great deal. Match your effort to the consequence rather than checking everything equally.

Better questions also reduce how much you have to catch later. Asking the model to flag which parts it is unsure about, and to say plainly when it does not know, changes the output more than most beginners expect. ## When this habit is not enough

**Verification only works when you can reach a real source. For medical, legal, tax, or safety decisions, a one minute check is the wrong tool, and a human expert is the right one. The same goes for anything where being wrong is expensive, slow to undo, or affects someone else.**

There is a second limit worth naming. Verifying often means pasting context back in, and what you paste is a separate question from whether the answer is right. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does turning on web search fix wrong answers?

It helps a lot with recency and citations, because the model can quote a page instead of reconstructing one. It does not remove the problem. The model still summarizes what it fetched, and summaries can misstate a source or lean on a weak page. Click the link and read the sentence it came from.

### Why does it sometimes give me a different answer to the same question?

Because generation involves an element of randomness, and small changes in phrasing shift what looks plausible. Consistency across fresh sessions is a rough signal of a solid fact. Variation on a specific number or name is a strong signal to verify it.

### Is a newer or paid model more accurate?

Generally yes on hard reasoning, and stronger models are better at saying they are unsure. The underlying method is the same, so the same failure mode remains. Upgrading reduces frequency, not category. Keep the check regardless of which model you use.

### Should I just tell it not to make things up?

Asking for uncertainty flags genuinely helps, and asking it to say "I do not know" makes that outcome more likely. It is not a guarantee, because the instruction is another pattern to follow rather than a switch that connects the model to the truth.

### How do I know when an answer is safe to use as is?

When nothing in it is a checkable specific, or when every specific is one you already know. Explanations, drafts, outlines, and brainstorms are usually safe. The moment a number, name, date, or citation appears and it matters, run the three questions.
