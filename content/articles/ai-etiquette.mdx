**AI etiquette comes down to four calls: disclose when someone is judging your effort or your judgment, ask before a notetaker joins a call, never automate grief or apology, and answer the person rather than the machine when you receive something obviously AI-written.**

Nobody handed you a rulebook for this. The rules are forming right now, in real offices, one awkward moment at a time. What follows is not manners advice. It is a set of concrete calls you can make today, with the reasoning attached so you can adapt them when your situation is different.

## When you owe a colleague a disclosure

**Disclose when the other person is evaluating you, when they will act on the content without checking it, or when they asked for your personal voice. Skip the disclosure for routine drafting help, the same way you skip crediting spellcheck. The test is what they are relying on, not which tool you happened to open.**

Most people get stuck because they ask the wrong question. "Did I use AI?" has no useful answer anymore, because the line between autocomplete, grammar tools, and a full draft is blurry and getting blurrier. The better question is: if this person found out later, would they feel misled about something that mattered to them?

That reframe sorts almost every case cleanly.

| Situation | Disclose? | The reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Draft email to a client, you reviewed and edited it | No | They rely on the message being accurate, not on who typed it |
| Written portion of a job application or performance self-review | Yes, if asked | The employer is evaluating your judgment and writing |
| A report a colleague will forward without reading closely | Yes | They are trusting your verification, and AI output can be confidently wrong |
| Meeting summary you did not read line by line | Yes | You are passing along claims you have not checked |
| Reference letter or peer feedback about a person | Yes | The value is your firsthand observation, not fluent prose |
| Slide headlines, formatting cleanup, rewording your own bullet points | No | Nobody was relying on you writing those by hand |

### The grey zone that trips people up

The hardest cases are the ones where you did real thinking and AI did the phrasing. A strategy memo where every idea is yours and every sentence is polished by a chatbot is, in most workplaces, fine and unremarkable. It becomes a problem the moment you have not verified a number, a name, or a claim inside it. That is a factual risk more than a social one, and it is worth understanding [why ChatGPT gives wrong answers so confidently](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) before you attach your name to anything it produced.

## Ask before a notetaker joins the call

**Ask in advance, in writing, before the bot appears in the waiting room. A recording assistant changes what people are willing to say, and springing one on a call is the modern equivalent of hitting record without telling anyone. One sentence in the calendar invite removes almost all of the friction.**

Here is the practical version. Put a line in the invite: "I plan to have a notetaker on this call for my own notes. Let me know if you would rather I take them by hand." That sentence does three things. It gives people a real out, it does it privately before the call rather than publicly during it, and it signals that you thought about it.

Two situations call for extra care. If anyone on the call is external, the recording may sit in a vendor account under terms they never agreed to. And if the conversation touches health details, salaries, legal matters, or anything about a specific named person who is not present, default to no bot. If you are unsure where your recordings and transcripts actually live, our plain-English walkthrough of [whether ChatGPT is confidential](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) covers the same questions that apply to notetakers.

## Why an AI-written condolence or apology reads as an insult

**Condolences and apologies are not information transfers. Their entire value is the cost you paid to write them: your attention, your discomfort, your memory of the person. Outsourcing that cost to software does not save time, it removes the only thing the message was carrying. The recipient can usually feel the absence even when they cannot name it.**

Think about what a sympathy note actually communicates. It is not "I am aware your father died." The recipient already knows. It is "I stopped what I was doing and sat with this." A fluent, well-structured, faintly generic paragraph says the opposite, and it says it loudly, because polished prose from someone who normally writes in fragments is a tell.

Apologies work the same way. An apology that arrives in perfect balanced prose reads as managed rather than felt, and it invites the reader to wonder whether you even reread it. The repair job after being caught is much harder than the original apology would have been.

The honest workaround: write three clumsy sentences yourself. "I heard about your mom. I remember you telling me about her garden. I am sorry." That is better than anything a model will produce, precisely because it is worse writing.

## What to do when you receive something obviously AI-written

**Respond to the substance, not the smell. Unless accuracy or effort was the point, calling it out costs you more than it gains, and you can be wrong. If it matters, ask a specific question about the content instead of making an accusation about the tool.**

Detection is genuinely unreliable, and the failure mode is ugly: accusing someone who wrote it themselves. Non-native English speakers and people who write formally get flagged constantly. If you want to understand what the real signals are and how weak they get, [our reader's guide to spotting AI writing](/articles/how-to-spot-ai-writing) is a fairer starting point than any detector score.

When it does matter, use this order:

1. Ask a content question. "Where did the 40 percent figure come from?" A person who did the work answers in a sentence. A person who did not will stall.
2. Ask about the process, not the tool. "How much of this did you get to verify?" That gives them a graceful path to say "less than I would like."
3. Only escalate if a real thing broke: a fabricated citation, an invented customer quote, a policy that does not exist.

For hiring specifically, expect AI-assisted applications and judge the substance instead. Our take on [using AI to write your resume](/articles/can-i-use-ai-to-write-my-resume) explains what still has to be true about the underlying claims.

## A safe exercise: draft your disclosure line this week

**Spend fifteen minutes writing your own reusable disclosure sentence before you need it under pressure. Having the words ready is what makes disclosure easy, and improvising one mid-conversation is what makes people avoid it entirely. Nothing here touches live work, so there is no risk in trying it.**

1. Pick a real message you sent recently with AI help. Do not resend it. Just look at it.
2. Run it against the table above. Did the recipient rely on your verification, your judgment, or your personal voice?
3. Write one sentence you would be comfortable adding. A working example: "I drafted this with AI and checked the numbers against the source file."
4. Save it somewhere you can find it. Reuse it unchanged. Consistency is what makes it read as normal rather than as a confession.

## Where this etiquette is still unsettled

**These norms are moving, and anyone claiming certainty is guessing. Disclosure expectations differ sharply by industry, country, and workplace culture, and what reads as honest transparency in one office reads as an admission of laziness in another. Treat the guidance here as a sensible default, not a settled standard.**

Two exceptions worth naming. In regulated fields such as law, medicine, and financial advice, your professional body may already have rules that outrank anything here, and those rules win. And in workplaces where AI use is quietly discouraged, disclosure carries a real career cost that this article cannot price for you. In that case the honest move is often to ask your manager what the expectation is, rather than deciding alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to tell my boss I used AI for a report?

If your boss will act on the report without independently checking the facts, say so, and say what you verified. If they are evaluating your thinking, say so. For routine formatting, summarizing your own notes, or tightening prose, no disclosure is expected in most workplaces.

### Is it rude to use a notetaker bot on a one-on-one call?

It is rude to add one without asking. Ask in the invite rather than at the top of the call, so declining stays private. On sensitive one-on-ones covering performance, pay, or health, skip the bot and take notes by hand.

### Can I use AI to help write a difficult personal message?

Use it to think, not to write. Asking a model what points to cover, or whether your draft sounds defensive, is reasonable. Sending its words as your own condolence or apology removes the effort that gave the message its meaning.

### What if I accuse someone of using AI and I am wrong?

That is the main reason not to accuse. Detectors are unreliable and misfire on formal writers and non-native speakers. Ask about the content instead: a specific question about a number or a source tells you what you need to know without the false-accusation risk.

### Should my team write down an AI policy?

A short one helps. Three lines covering notetakers, client-facing content, and what must be human-verified will resolve most disputes.
