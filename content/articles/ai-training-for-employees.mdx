**Train your team on their own work, not on a generic AI course. Pick one real recurring task per person, teach a single verification habit, and set one hard rule about what never gets pasted into a chatbot. Four weeks of thirty minute sessions built on existing work will do more than a paid program.**

## Why generic AI courses do not transfer to the job

**Most courses teach the tool in a vacuum. People finish knowing what a prompt is and still cannot say which of their Tuesday tasks to hand over. Transfer happens when the practice material is the actual work sitting in someone's inbox, with the real constraints, real names, and real deadline attached.**

Think about an office manager at a six person insurance agency. She sits through a two hour webinar on AI productivity, sees a demo about writing a blog post, and goes back to her desk where the actual work is renewal reminders, certificate requests, and a spreadsheet that never balances. Nothing in the webinar told her which of those three to try first, or what to do when the answer looks plausible and is wrong.

That gap is the whole problem. Skill with a chatbot is not general. It is task shaped. Someone can be genuinely good at using AI to draft client emails and completely lost using it on a spreadsheet, because the checking method is different in each case. Small teams outside tech run into this constantly, which is why [AI for Nonprofits: What a Small Team Can Safely Hand Off](/articles/ai-for-nonprofits) frames the decision as which tasks are safe to release rather than which features to learn.

## The three things that actually have to be taught

**Everything else is optional. A person is competent with AI at work when they have completed one real task end to end, they check output before it leaves the building, and they know exactly what they are never allowed to paste in. Teach those three and the rest gets picked up on the job.**

### One first real task per person

Not a demo. Not a sample. A task they already do every week that is low stakes if it comes out badly. Good candidates: turning meeting notes into an action list, rewriting a policy paragraph in plain language, summarizing a long email thread before forwarding it, drafting a first pass at a job posting.

Bad candidates for week one: anything that goes straight to a client, anything with money in it, anything involving a legal or medical decision. The point of the first task is to build the habit loop, not to save time yet. If you want a structure for picking, [Plan your first project](/modules/module-6/first-project) walks through choosing something small enough to finish.

### The verification habit

This is the part nobody teaches and everybody needs. The rule is simple to state: nothing leaves the building unchecked, and the person who ran the prompt is the person who checks it.

What checking means depends on the task. For a summary, read the original and confirm nothing important was dropped or invented. For a spreadsheet formula, run it on three rows where you already know the answer. For anything with a name, date, dollar figure, or citation in it, verify each one against a source you trust. Explaining why this matters is easier when the team understands the mechanism, and [Why Does ChatGPT Give Wrong Answers? A Plain-English Answer](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) covers that in language a nontechnical person can follow in one sitting.

### The paste rule

Write one sentence and put it where people can see it. Something like: no client names, no account or card numbers, no medical or HR details, no passwords, no unreleased financials go into any AI tool.

Then teach the workaround, because a rule with no workaround gets broken quietly. The workaround is substitution. Replace the client name with "the client," replace the account number with "the account number," run the task, then put the real details back in yourself. Ninety percent of sensitive tasks survive this fine. The other ten percent were never a good fit. There is also a disclosure question that comes up fast once people start using AI on shared work, and [AI Etiquette: The Unwritten Rules, Answered Plainly](/articles/ai-etiquette) is a reasonable thing to circulate before someone gets it wrong in front of a client.

## A four-week plan built on work your team already does

**Four sessions, thirty minutes each, same time every week. No slides. Everyone brings their own task and works on it in the room. Your job in each session is to ask one question and listen to the answer. The plan below assumes six people and no budget.**

| Week | Focus | What the team does | What you check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One real task | Each person names a recurring task they do weekly and runs it once with AI help | Did they pick something low stakes and repeatable |
| 2 | Verification | Same task again, with a written check step before anything is sent | Can they show you what they checked and how |
| 3 | Sensitive details | Rewrite the same task with names, numbers, and client details removed | Did anything private survive into the prompt |
| 4 | Handoff | Write the task up as a short recipe someone else could follow | Would a new hire get the same result from it |

By week four you have six written recipes that are specific to your business. That artifact is the actual output of the training. ## A safe exercise for week one

**Use one exercise for the whole team so you can compare results. The meeting notes rewrite works because everyone has notes, nothing confidential has to appear, and the failure mode is visible immediately. Budget twenty minutes and do it live rather than assigning it as homework.**

1. Each person opens notes from a recent internal meeting. Internal only, no client meetings this week.
2. Strip out names. Replace them with "the manager," "the vendor," "the requester."
3. Paste the notes and ask for two things: a list of decisions made, and a list of open action items with who owns each one.
4. Read the original notes again and mark anything the AI added that was not there. This is the important step. It will find something.
5. Fix the output by hand and send it to the team as the real meeting summary.

Step four is where the learning lives. When someone sees an invented action item in their own notes, the abstract warning about AI making things up becomes a concrete thing they now watch for. Pair this with [Prompt Engineering for Beginners: A Plain-English Start](/articles/prompt-engineering-for-beginners) for anyone who wants to go deeper on how to ask.

## When this plan is the wrong call

**Skip it if your work is regulated in a way that makes the paste rule impossible to honor, or if nobody on the team has thirty spare minutes a week. Both are real. A training plan that competes with billable hours loses, and a plan that quietly encourages rule breaking is worse than nothing.**

Two other honest limits. First, a four week plan produces beginners who are careful, not experts. Expect first drafts and time savings on routine writing, not a redesigned workflow. Second, some tasks will not survive contact with substitution, particularly anything where the sensitive detail is the whole point. Those tasks stay manual, and saying so out loud early keeps the rest of the plan credible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to buy anything to run this?

No. The free tier of a mainstream assistant is enough for four weeks of drafting and summarizing work. Pay only when a specific person hits a specific limit, and let them tell you which limit it was.

### What if someone on the team refuses to use it?

Let them sit out week one. Requiring enthusiasm produces fake compliance. What you can require is that they know the paste rule, since that protects the business whether or not they personally use the tool.

### How do I know it worked?

Look for the written recipes from week four and for people checking output without being reminded. Time saved is a poor early measure because week one is usually slower than doing the task by hand.

### Should I set a written policy first?

One sentence is enough to start, covering what never gets pasted in. A longer policy written before anyone has used the tool tends to ban things nobody was going to do and miss the thing they actually did.

### What comes after four weeks?

Pick one task that is now boring and reliable, and ask whether it should be automated rather than assisted. Start that conversation with [Do you actually need AI?](/modules/module-5/ai-or-automation) rather than adding another tool by reflex.
