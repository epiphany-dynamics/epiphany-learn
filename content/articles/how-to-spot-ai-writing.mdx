**You spot AI writing by structure, not by vibes. Look for even sentence rhythm, hedging in every paragraph, and specifics that dissolve when you ask one follow-up question. Detector scores are guesses, not proof. The reliable test is asking the named author to explain a choice only the real writer could explain.**

Most people arrive at this question with a document already in hand: a job application, a student essay, a report from a contractor, a proposal from a vendor. Something about it reads smooth and empty at the same time. This guide is the reading skill for that moment, in plain English, and then the same skill turned around on your own drafts.

## What AI writing actually looks like on the page

**Most AI drafts share four habits: sentences of nearly equal length, a balanced tone that never commits, confident nouns with no verifiable detail behind them, and tidy summary sentences that repeat the paragraph above. None of these prove anything alone. Two or three stacked in one short document is the real signal.**

### Sentence rhythm that never varies

Human writing bunches up. People write a long tangled sentence, then a short one, then a fragment, because they are thinking while typing. A generated draft tends to hold a steady medium length for paragraph after paragraph. Read three paragraphs aloud. If your breathing pattern never changes, note it.

### Hedging in every direction at once

Generated prose is trained to be agreeable, so it often says a thing and its opposite in the same breath: important for many organizations, though results may vary depending on your situation. A person with a stake in the outcome usually picks a side somewhere, even quietly.

### Specificity that evaporates under one question

This is the strongest tell. The draft names a process, a benefit, a timeline. Ask what happened in week two, and there is nothing underneath. Real authors carry details they did not use, because they lived the thing they are describing.

| Tell | What you notice | Weak on its own because | Stronger when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Even rhythm | Paragraphs feel metronomic | Trained business writers do this too | Paired with no concrete detail |
| Blanket hedging | Every claim is softened | Careful people hedge honestly | The hedges protect nothing specific |
| Hollow specifics | Named results, no source | Confidentiality can explain gaps | One follow-up question finds nothing |
| Closing echo | Last line restates the paragraph | Some templates require it | It happens in every section |

## Why a detector score is not evidence

**An AI detector returns a probability, not a finding of fact. It can flag careful non-native English, technical writing, and edited-to-death corporate prose as machine written, and it can miss a lightly rewritten AI draft entirely. Treat a score as a reason to read closely, never as the reason for an accusation.**

Detectors work by measuring how predictable the word choices are. The trouble is that predictable is also what clear, plain, heavily edited writing looks like. A person writing in their second language often reaches for the most standard phrasing available, and that reads as predictable too.

The other direction fails just as easily. Ask a model to write in short punchy sentences with one odd word per paragraph, and the score moves. A tool that can be steered by a single instruction is not a tool you should hang a decision on.

Useful framing: a detector output is a smoke alarm, not a verdict. It tells you where to look. What you find when you look is the actual evidence.

## The one question that separates a real author from a pasted draft

**Ask the writer why they left something out. A real author can name the paragraph they cut, the claim they could not verify, or the reader they were trying not to annoy. Someone who pasted a draft can only re-describe what is already on the page, usually in the same words.**

The question works because generated text has no history. It was not chosen from among rejected options, so there is nothing behind it to recall.

Good versions of the question, adapted to your situation:

1. What did you take out of this, and why did it not make the cut?
2. Which part are you least confident about?
3. If you had one more day, what would you go verify first?
4. Who is going to disagree with this, and where?

Listen for texture, not fluency. An author says something like "I dropped the pricing table because our numbers change in October and I did not want it in writing." A person reading their own pasted draft says "I focused on the key points to keep it concise." That second answer is a description of the document, not a memory of writing it.

## A safe exercise you can run this week

**Run a blind read on writing that carries no stakes. Take three short pieces you wrote yourself, three you know were AI generated, and mix them. Score each one against the same checklist before you look at the answers. You will learn where your instincts are sharp and where they are noise.**

How to set it up:

1. Pick six short items, roughly 200 words each: old emails, notes, blog intros.
2. Generate three of them yourself with any assistant, on topics you know.
3. Strip names and dates so you cannot cheat on context.
4. Shuffle, then read each one cold.
5. Score each on four points: rhythm varies, takes a position, contains a checkable detail, ends without echoing itself.
6. Reveal the answers and see which tells actually predicted correctly for you.

Do this with your own material, not with somebody else's confidential document. Pasting a client file or a personnel record into a consumer chat tool is a separate risk with its own rules, covered in [our plain-English answer on whether ChatGPT is confidential](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential).

## How to keep your own AI-assisted writing from reading that way

**Use the model for drafting and structure, then put yourself back in. Add one detail only you know, break the sentence rhythm on purpose, cut every hedge you cannot defend, and take a position in at least one sentence. The goal is not to hide the tool. It is to make the writing yours.**

A short pass that works on almost any draft:

- Replace one generic claim with a specific thing that happened, including the awkward part.
- Delete the final sentence of every section if it only restates the section.
- Find three softeners (often, typically, may) and either cut them or justify them.
- Say one thing you would defend in a meeting.
- Read it aloud once and fix wherever your voice flattens out.

Better input reduces how much repair you need. A prompt that carries your constraints, audience, and real examples produces a draft that already sounds closer to you, which is the practical point of [our beginner five-part prompt formula](/articles/how-to-write-ai-prompts).

## When not to use these signals

**Do not use these tells to accuse a person. Structural signals describe a document, not an intent, and the cost of being wrong lands on a student, an employee, or a job applicant who cannot appeal. Use the reading skill to decide how much to trust a document, and use conversation to decide about people.**

There is also an honest limit on the tells themselves: they were built by reading yesterday's models. Output changes, and a well-edited AI draft can pass every check on this page. Hold your conclusions loosely.

The durable move is to stop asking who typed it and start asking whether the claims hold. That question never goes out of date. When you are ready to apply this judgment to real work, [plan your first AI project](/modules/module-6/first-project) with a task where you can check the output yourself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I tell for certain that something was written by AI?

No. You can build a reasoned level of confidence from several signals at once, but certainty is not available from reading alone. Treat your read as one input, alongside the author's ability to discuss their own choices and whether the factual claims survive checking.

### Are AI detectors ever worth using?

As a triage tool for your own attention, sometimes. As justification for a grade, a firing, or a rejection, no. They produce probabilities on text patterns and have documented trouble with non-native English and formal writing, so a score should never be the last word.

### Does using AI to write mean the work is dishonest?

Not by itself. The problem is unverified claims and absent judgment, not the tool. If a person directed the work, checked the facts, and can defend the choices, that is authorship. Our [prompt engineering starter guide](/articles/prompt-engineering-for-beginners) covers doing that deliberately.

### What if the writer refuses to answer questions about their draft?

Refusal is information, but it is not proof. People get defensive when they feel accused, including people who wrote every word. Ask about the content rather than the process: what the numbers mean, where they came from, what happens if the main assumption is wrong.
