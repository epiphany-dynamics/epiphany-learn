**AI helps real estate agents most on drafting and explaining: first-pass listing copy, follow-up sequences, plain-language summaries of a disclosure or an inspection report. It breaks on pricing judgment, negotiation, and anything touching fair housing, where a confident guess turns into a legal problem. Draft with it, decide without it.**

## What an assistant genuinely absorbs in your week

**The reliable wins are language tasks where you already know the answer and just need it written faster. Listing copy drafts, follow-up sequences, plain-English explanations of jargon, and summaries of documents you hand it. In each case you supply the facts and the judgment, and the model supplies sentences.**

Think about the parts of your week that are typing rather than deciding. You already know the house has a new roof and an awkward third bedroom. Writing that up for the fifth time this month is not skill, it is repetition. That is the part an assistant absorbs cleanly.

### Listing copy, first draft only

Give it the facts you would tell a colleague out loud, then ask for three versions at different lengths. You will keep about half of one and rewrite the rest, which is still faster than a blank page.

### Follow-up sequences

A five-message sequence for people who toured but went quiet is a genuinely good use. You write the strategy, the model writes the variations, and you edit the voice back in so it sounds like you and not like a template.

### Explaining and summarising

Paste a dense paragraph from a disclosure and ask for a version a first-time buyer can follow. Ask for a summary of an inspection report grouped by urgency. The document is the source of truth, and you check the summary against it before it goes anywhere.

## What breaks when AI tries to do the job

**Anything that requires judgment about a specific market, a specific seller, or a specific person breaks. The model has no live MLS access, no feel for how a street is trading this month, and no stake in the outcome. It answers confidently anyway, which is exactly what makes those failures easy to miss.**

Pricing is the clearest example. Ask a chatbot what a house is worth and you will get a number with reasoning attached. The reasoning is fluent, the number is invented. It does not have your recent comparable sales, it does not know the two pending deals that reset the block, and it cannot walk the property. A comparative market analysis is your professional opinion, and putting a machine guess behind it is a liability you do not need.

Negotiation is the second. The model has no read on the other agent, no sense of how motivated the seller actually is, and no memory of the phone call where something shifted. It will produce a reasonable-sounding counter that ignores the one detail the deal turns on.

Then there are plain factual errors. Square footage, school ratings, HOA fees, tax figures, and permit history are all things a model will fill in smoothly when it does not know. If you have not read [Why Does ChatGPT Give Wrong Answers? A Plain-English Answer](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers), it is worth ten minutes, because the failure mode is confidence rather than obvious nonsense.

## The fair housing line, in plain terms

**The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability. A language model has no concept of that line. It writes what sounds warm and appealing, and warm appealing phrasing is where fair housing violations usually hide. This is the compliance issue, not a footnote.**

Ask for "friendly, inviting" listing copy and you will often get phrases that describe the buyer instead of the property: perfect for a young family, ideal for empty nesters, a quiet building with mature residents, walking distance to St. Mary's, a safe neighborhood. Every one of those describes who should live there. That is the pattern regulators look for, and it does not matter that a model wrote it. Your name is on the listing.

The same risk shows up in buyer conversations. If someone asks which neighborhood is best for a family like theirs, a chatbot will happily answer. That answer is steering. You redirect to objective data and let the buyer decide, exactly as you would without a machine in the room.

Practical guardrail: put the constraint in the prompt every time. Something like "Describe only the property and its features. Do not describe or imply anything about who would live here. Do not mention schools, churches, safety, family suitability, or demographics." Then read the output as if a compliance officer were reading it, because eventually one might. When you are unsure, ask your broker or your association's compliance contact. That is a question for a person, not a model.

## A task-by-task split you can print

**Use the table below as a default, not a rule. The pattern is simple: hand over tasks where you already hold the facts and the model only shapes the words, and keep tasks where the answer itself is the professional product. Anything in the middle gets drafted by AI and verified line by line.**

| Task | Hand to AI? | What you still have to do |
|---|---|---|
| Listing description draft | Yes, first draft | Verify every fact, strip buyer-targeting language |
| Follow-up email or text sequence | Yes | Set the strategy, edit into your voice, honor opt-outs |
| Explaining a disclosure clause to a client | Yes, as a starting point | Check against the actual document, flag anything legal |
| Summarising an inspection report | Yes, with the file provided | Read the report yourself before you repeat the summary |
| Social captions and open house blurbs | Yes | Same fair housing review as listing copy |
| Pricing or a CMA | No | Your own comps and judgment |
| Negotiation strategy or counters | No | You, on the phone |
| Advising which neighborhood suits a buyer | Never | Objective data only, buyer decides |
| Anything about a client's finances or identity | Careful | See privacy note below |

On privacy: a client's inspection report, pre-approval letter, or offer terms are their information, not yours to feed into whatever tool is open. Check your brokerage policy first, and read [Is ChatGPT Confidential? A Plain-English Answer](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) so you understand what happens to what you paste.

## A safe exercise: rewrite one disclosure paragraph

**Spend fifteen minutes on a task with no client attached. Take one paragraph of a real disclosure or contract, ask for a plain-English version, then check it against the original. You will learn more about where the tool helps and where it drifts than from any list of tips.**

1. Pick one dense paragraph from a document you already understand well.
2. Prompt it plainly: "Rewrite this for a first-time buyer with no real estate background. Keep every condition and deadline. Do not add anything that is not in the text. List anything ambiguous separately."
3. Read the output against the original, sentence by sentence.
4. Mark three things: what it explained better than you would have, what it quietly dropped, and anything it added that was not there.
5. Save the prompt that worked. That is your reusable version.

Most agents find it genuinely good at the explaining and slightly loose with deadlines and conditions, which tells you exactly which parts to check forever after. ## When not to use AI at all

**Skip it when the stakes are legal, when the answer is your professional opinion, or when a client is upset. A distressed seller does not want a well-structured message. They want you. Some parts of this job are the relationship, and outsourcing the words is how you lose it.**

Also skip it when you cannot check the output. If you do not know enough to catch an error in a summary, the summary is not saving you time, it is hiding risk. And do not paste AI text straight into a client conversation without reading it aloud first; [AI Etiquette: The Unwritten Rules, Answered Plainly](/articles/ai-etiquette) covers when to say a tool was involved and when nobody needs to know.

One honest caveat: none of this is legal advice, and brokerage and MLS rules on AI-generated content vary and are changing. Ask your broker before you build a workflow around it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can AI write my listing descriptions?

It can write the first draft. It should not write the final version. You provide the facts, review every number, and remove any phrasing that describes the ideal buyer rather than the property. Expect to keep roughly half of what it gives you, which is still faster than starting cold.

### Will AI replace real estate agents?

Not on the parts clients pay for. Pricing judgment, negotiation, and being the person who answers the phone during a bad inspection are not language tasks. What changes is that the writing and admin layer gets thinner, so the agents who use it well get more hours back for the work that closes deals.

### Is it safe to upload a client's documents to a chatbot?

Check your brokerage policy first, then check the tool's settings. Consumer chat tools differ in whether they train on what you type, and some let you turn that off. Treat client financials and identity documents as off limits until you have confirmed both.

### What is the actual fair housing risk?

Language models optimize for appealing copy, and appealing copy often describes people. Phrases like "great for families" or "safe neighborhood" can be read as targeting protected classes. You are responsible for what publishes under your name, so review every generated line with that specifically in mind.
