**Never paste customer data, passwords or credentials, unpublished financial numbers, or personnel files into ChatGPT at work. Anything you type may be stored, reviewed, or leaked, and your employer may see it. This page gives you a concrete do-not-paste list, a safe rewriting method, and a practice exercise you can run in five minutes.**

## The short list: never paste these four things

**The four categories you should never paste are customer data, credentials, unpublished numbers, and personnel files. If a snippet of text would cause real harm if it appeared on a public website tomorrow, it does not belong in a chatbot prompt. When in doubt, leave it out and rewrite around it.**

Here is the list in concrete terms. Keep it near your desk until it becomes habit.

1. **Customer data.** Names, email addresses, phone numbers, home addresses, order histories, support transcripts, health details, or anything that identifies a real person you serve. Even a single name attached to a complaint can identify someone.
2. **Credentials.** Passwords, API keys, access tokens, recovery codes, license keys, and login URLs. Treat anything that opens a door as radioactive. This includes credentials you find inside code or config files you are tempted to paste for debugging.
3. **Unpublished numbers.** Revenue figures before earnings are announced, draft budgets, salary bands, pricing under negotiation, contract values, and acquisition talks. In many industries, sharing these early is not just careless; it can violate securities rules or confidentiality agreements.
4. **Personnel files.** Performance notes about named colleagues, disciplinary records, medical or accommodation details, hiring decisions before they are announced, and anything from an HR system. These belong to the people they describe, not to you.

A useful mental test: if you would not be comfortable reading it aloud in a public meeting, do not paste it into a chat window either.

## Why the risk is real, not theoretical

**ChatGPT is a cloud service run by another company. Your prompts travel to their servers, may be kept for a period under their data policies, and in some setups may be reviewed by humans or used to improve the service. Once text leaves your machine, you no longer control where it goes or who eventually reads it.**

This is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to treat a chatbot the way you treat any outside vendor: helpful, but not inside your walls. Three things follow.

First, your company's data rules apply to chatbots the same way they apply to email and file sharing. If your policy forbids sending customer lists to an outside contractor, pasting those lists into ChatGPT is the same category of problem.

Second, account settings and plan types change what happens to your data, and those details differ between personal and business plans. Our plain-English explainer on [whether ChatGPT is confidential](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) walks through the moving parts in calm detail.

Third, if you are using a work computer or a work account, your employer may have visibility into what you do. If that possibility matters to you, read [Can My Employer See My ChatGPT History? An Honest Answer](/articles/can-my-employer-see-my-chatgpt-history) before assuming anything is private.

## A do-not-paste checklist you can scan in ten seconds

**Before you press enter on any work prompt, scan it against five questions: does it name a customer, contain a secret, include unpublished numbers, describe a named colleague, or reveal strategy? One yes means stop and rewrite. This takes ten seconds once it becomes routine.**

| Check | Ask yourself | If yes |
|---|---|---|
| Customer data | Does this name, identify, or describe a real customer or client? | Remove or anonymize it |
| Credentials | Is there a password, key, token, or login anywhere in the text? | Delete it entirely |
| Unpublished numbers | Are these figures public yet? Would finance approve sharing them? | Replace with placeholders |
| Personnel | Does this describe a named person's performance, health, or pay? | Generalize or do not send |
| Strategy | Would a competitor benefit from reading this? | Abstract the details |

The strategy row is the one people forget. A prompt like "our top client is threatening to leave over the Q3 price increase, draft a retention email" tells an outside service who your client is, that they are unhappy, and what your pricing did. That is competitive intelligence, handed over for free.

## How to get help anyway: the rewrite method

**You rarely need the real data to get real help. Replace names with roles, swap exact figures for rounded illustrative ones, and describe the structure of your problem instead of its contents. ChatGPT answers the shape of a question just as well without the sensitive parts.**

Here is the method in three moves.

1. **Substitute roles for names.** "Our largest retail client" instead of the company name. "A senior engineer on my team" instead of the person's name.
2. **Substitute ranges for exact numbers.** "A contract worth roughly six figures" instead of $187,400. Illustrative numbers keep the advice realistic without leaking anything.
3. **Describe the structure, not the content.** Instead of pasting a confidential contract clause, write "a clause that limits our liability but allows the client to terminate with 30 days notice" and ask about drafting approaches in general.

Worked example. Instead of: "Write an apology email to Dana Whitfield at Riverbend Logistics about the invoice error of $4,230 on account RL-8817." Write: "Write an apology email to a long-standing logistics client about a billing error of a few thousand dollars. Tone: accountable, brief, no excuses." The second version gets you a draft that is 90 percent done, and you fill in the real details yourself afterward.

## A safe five-minute exercise

**Practice the rewrite method on something harmless today. Take an old, already-resolved piece of work with no live sensitivity, write the prompt you would naturally type, then rewrite it using the three substitution moves. Comparing the two versions builds the habit faster than any policy document.**

Here is a concrete exercise you can do right now.

1. Think of a task you finished last month: a tricky email, a summary, a plan. Nothing current, nothing involving active clients.
2. Write the prompt you would have pasted in the moment, with all the real details. Do not send it. Just write it in a note on your computer.
3. Now rewrite it: roles instead of names, ranges instead of figures, structure instead of content.
4. Paste only the rewritten version into ChatGPT and read the answer.
5. Ask yourself: was the answer meaningfully worse than it would have been with the real details? For most drafting, brainstorming, and structure questions, it will not be.

## One honest limitation of this approach

**Anonymizing prompts reduces risk; it does not eliminate it. Reassembly is the failure mode: several harmless-seeming details combined can still identify a client or situation, and your employer's policy may forbid chatbot use entirely regardless of what you paste. When a rule or contract says no, that answer wins.**

Two specific exceptions deserve attention.

First, small worlds make anonymization leaky. If you work in a niche industry and write "our largest client, a regional hospital network in the Pacific Northwest," anyone motivated could narrow that down fast. The more distinctive the situation, the more aggressively you need to generalize.

Second, some workplaces and some data types are simply off-limits no matter how careful you are. Regulated health information, classified work, and anything under a strict NDA may have a blanket rule: nothing goes into outside tools, period. If your role touches data like this, ask your compliance or IT team for the actual policy before using any chatbot for work tasks. A checklist from a website, including this one, is not a substitute for your organization's rules.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it ever safe to paste work documents into ChatGPT?

Sometimes, if the document contains nothing from the do-not-paste list and your employer's policy allows it. Public-facing drafts, generic templates, and your own notes with no sensitive details are usually lower risk. When you are unsure whether a document qualifies, leave it out and describe its structure instead.

### What if I already pasted something sensitive?

Delete the conversation if your version allows it, change any exposed passwords or credentials immediately, and tell your manager or IT contact what happened. Reporting early is almost always treated far better than staying quiet, and credentials should be rotated regardless of what happens next.

### Does deleting a chat remove the data from OpenAI's servers?

Not necessarily, and not immediately. Deletion removes the conversation from your view, but retention depends on your plan type and the provider's current policies. This is why the rule is do-not-paste rather than paste-then-delete.

### Are business or enterprise ChatGPT plans safer for work data?

They generally come with stronger data handling terms, such as prompts not being used for training by default, but they do not make sensitive data sharing harmless. Company policy, contracts, and regulations still apply. Check with your IT team about which plan, if any, is approved at your workplace.

### What can I safely use ChatGPT for at work?

Plenty: drafting generic emails, brainstorming, explaining concepts, outlining documents, rewording your own non-sensitive writing, and practicing conversations. The pattern is simple. Use it for thinking and structure, then add the real names, numbers, and details yourself in your own documents.
