**ChatGPT is useful to an executive assistant for drafting, summarizing, and structuring: agendas, briefing packs, travel comparisons, email drafts, and action lists. It is not safe for raw inbox contents, calendar exports, passport details, or board material. Anonymize first, paste second, and keep the priority call with the human who knows the room.**

## What ChatGPT is good at in an assistant's week

**The reliable wins are shaping and shortening text you already have: turning messy notes into an agenda, compressing six travel options into a comparison, drafting a polite decline, and pulling action items out of a meeting summary. Each of these has a clear input, a clear output, and a human who reads it before it ships.**

The pattern that works is narrow and boring. You bring the facts. The model brings structure, tone, and speed. It does not know your executive, your board, or which vendor is quietly on thin ice, so it should never be the one deciding what matters.

Here is a working split of common tasks. The task list is drawn from ordinary assistant work, not from a study, so treat it as a starting frame rather than a benchmark.

| Task | What you paste | What comes back | Who checks it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly agenda | Anonymized bullet notes | Ordered agenda with time blocks | You, for priority order |
| Briefing pack | Role labels plus neutral facts | One page summary, three questions to ask | You, for accuracy and tone |
| Travel options | Prices, times, layovers, no booking codes | Side by side comparison table | You, for policy fit |
| Email draft | Purpose, tone, key points | Two or three drafts to pick from | You, always, before sending |
| Action list | Sanitized meeting notes | Owners, tasks, due dates | The meeting owner |

If your week also includes expense reconciliation or a tracker that keeps breaking, the same care applies to spreadsheet work. ## What stays out of the chat box entirely

**Assume anything you paste could be read by someone who should not read it. That rules out raw inbox threads, calendar exports, passport and visa scans, loyalty numbers, home addresses, salary or severance details, legal correspondence, and board material. Executive names and company names come out too, replaced with neutral labels.**

The reason is simple. An assistant sits on top of information that is valuable precisely because it is not public: who the executive is meeting before an announcement, which director is unhappy, when the family travels. None of that needs to enter a general purpose tool to get a good agenda back.

Three habits cover most of the risk:

- Replace names with roles. "Exec A", "CFO", "Vendor 1", "Board member 2".
- Strip identifiers. No record locators, account numbers, employee IDs, or street addresses.
- Keep the shape, drop the specifics. "Quarterly review with a regional lead" carries enough meaning to build an agenda.

## The anonymized briefing pack workflow

**A briefing pack is where anonymization pays off most, because the raw material is almost always sensitive. The method is to build a sanitized fact sheet by hand, let the model shape it into a readable page, then re-insert the real names yourself in your own document at the very end.**

1. Open a blank document and write your fact sheet with labels instead of names. "Exec A meets Vendor 1 on Thursday. Vendor 1 has missed two delivery dates. Contract renews in six weeks."
2. Remove anything that identifies the parties: company, city, product name, contract value if it is unusual enough to be recognizable.
3. Paste the sanitized sheet into ChatGPT and ask for a one page briefing with three sections: context, decisions needed, questions to ask.
4. Read the draft against your sheet. Delete any claim you did not supply. Models fill gaps confidently, and an invented history is worse than a thin one.
5. Copy the structure into your own document and swap the labels back to real names there.
6. Add the part only you can add: the tone note. "Exec A prefers numbers first, no preamble."

Steps 3 and 4 are the whole job. If a fact appears in the draft that was not in your input, that is your signal to slow down and check every other line.

There will be times a document is genuinely safe to hand over, such as a published annual report or a public conference schedule. ## A safe exercise you can run in thirty minutes

**Practice on a company that does not exist. Invent an executive, a vendor, and a trip, then run the full briefing workflow end to end. You get the muscle memory without any exposure, and you find out quickly where the model guesses instead of reporting.**

Try this:

1. Write eight bullets about "Exec A" at a fictional 200 person logistics firm: one board meeting, one vendor issue, one delayed hire, one trip to a city you pick.
2. Ask for a one page briefing pack with context, decisions, and questions.
3. Ask for three flight options summarized as a table, giving it the times and prices yourself.
4. Ask for a two sentence email declining a speaking invitation, warm but final.
5. Mark every sentence in the output that you did not supply. Count them.

That count is the number you carry into real work. It tells you how closely you need to read before anything reaches your executive.

## Where human context still decides priority

**A model can order an agenda by stated urgency. It cannot know that the quiet item from the operations lead outranks the loud one from sales because of a conversation last quarter. Sequencing, tone, and who gets the executive's attention remain judgment calls that live with you.**

Three places where the human call is not optional:

- **Order.** Two items both look urgent on paper. You know which one the executive will be asked about first.
- **Tone.** A decline that reads as clean and professional to a model can read as cold to a person who has been waiting three weeks for a reply.
- **Omission.** Sometimes the right move is leaving an item off the agenda entirely because the timing is wrong. No prompt will surface that.

Treat the output as a well organized first draft from a capable temp who started this morning and has never met anyone in the building.

## When this is the wrong tool

**Skip it when anonymizing would strip the meaning, when your employer has a policy against external AI tools, when the material is legal, medical, or pre announcement, or when you are in a live crisis and a second draft costs more time than writing it yourself. Those cases are common enough to name up front.**

Board material deserves a special mention. Even anonymized, the combination of a date, a decision, and a dollar figure can be identifying. If you cannot describe the situation to a stranger in a coffee shop without them working out who it is, it does not belong in the chat box either.

Check your organization's policy before you start, not after. If there is no written policy, ask for one in writing. That answer protects you far more than a good prompt does.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I paste an email thread if I remove the names?

Usually not enough. Threads carry signatures, quoted addresses, project codenames, and phrasing that identifies the writer. Summarize the thread in your own neutral words instead, then ask for a reply draft based on your summary.

### Is it safe to use ChatGPT for my executive's calendar?

Not with the live calendar. Exported calendars reveal meeting partners, travel windows, and personal appointments. You can safely ask for help designing an agenda template or a meeting cadence, using generic slot labels rather than real entries.

### Will my executive notice the drafts are AI assisted?

They might, if you ship the first output. Models default to a padded, hedging register. Cut the preamble, shorten the sentences, and add one specific detail only you know. That single edit does most of the work of sounding like you.

### What if the briefing pack contains something I never told it?

Delete it and re-check the rest of the draft. Invented details are the main failure mode of this workflow, which is why step 4 exists. A briefing pack with a fabricated date is worse than no briefing pack at all.

### Where should I start if I have thirty minutes?

Run the fictional company exercise above. It is the fastest way to see both the value and the failure mode without risking anything real, and it gives you a template you can reuse every week afterward.
