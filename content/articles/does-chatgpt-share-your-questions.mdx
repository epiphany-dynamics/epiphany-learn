**By default, ChatGPT does not share your questions with other users or the public. Some of the provider's own staff can read them under stated conditions, such as abuse review or support, and consumer chats may be used for training unless you opt out. What your company's plan allows changes the answer, so check it.**

If you have ever paused before pasting a work email into a chatbot, that instinct is a good one. This guide explains who can actually see what you type, what "training" means, and why the honest answer depends on the plan behind your login. It also gives you a small exercise to build a safe habit.

One note on certainty: privacy policies and settings change. Everything below describes the general pattern, not a promise. Before you rely on it for anything sensitive, read the current policy yourself.

## Who can read what you type

**Other users cannot see your chats. The provider's staff can, but only under stated conditions, such as investigating abuse, fixing a problem you reported, or meeting a legal duty. Your employer may also see them if you use a work account. Policies change, so treat this as a snapshot.**

It helps to picture four groups of possible readers.

### Other people using ChatGPT

Your conversation is not a public post. Someone else typing a question does not get a window into yours. The exception is when you deliberately create a share link to a chat. Anyone with that link can open it, so only make one for content you would be happy to hand out.

### The provider's staff

Providers say that a limited number of authorized employees, and sometimes trusted contractors, may look at conversations in specific cases. Typical reasons are checking for misuse, investigating a security problem, helping with a support request, or responding to a legal demand. That is not the same as staff casually reading everything. It also is not the same as nobody ever reading anything, and the honest version of the answer includes both halves.

### Your employer

If you log in with a work account on a business plan, an administrator may have tools to manage the workspace, and in some setups to search or export conversations for compliance. If you use a personal account on a work laptop, your employer's device monitoring is a separate matter that ChatGPT's policy cannot describe.

### Anyone who gets into your account

A stolen password or a shared login exposes your history. Strong passwords and two-step sign-in matter more here than any privacy toggle.

## Why training is not the same as sharing

**Training means your text may help improve future models. It does not mean a stranger can pull up your conversation. Still, on personal plans, training is often on unless you switch it off in settings, so the safe habit is to check the data controls rather than assume.**

People often merge two different worries into one. The first is "will someone read my question?" The second is "will my question shape the tool for everyone?" They are separate.

When a provider trains on conversations, the goal is to improve how the model responds in general. Providers describe steps to reduce personal details in that process, but you should not treat any of that as a guarantee. A model can occasionally produce text that resembles material it learned from, which is one reason many companies prefer not to let staff paste in anything confidential.

Most personal accounts have a setting in the data controls area that lets you decline training use. There is also usually a temporary chat mode that is not saved to your history. The exact wording and location of these options change, so look in your own settings rather than trusting a screenshot from a year ago.

## How the plan your company bought changes the answer

**Business plans generally promise more than personal ones: no training on your data by default and admin controls. Free and personal plans promise less. Which one you are on depends on how you log in, and only your company's admin or contract can confirm what applies to you.**

This is the part most articles skip. "Is ChatGPT private?" has no single answer because the same website serves very different products. The table shows the general pattern. Treat the cells as things to verify, not guarantees.

| Situation | Used for training by default? | Who else may have access | What to verify |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free or personal account | Often yes, with an opt-out | Provider staff under stated conditions | Your data controls setting |
| Personal paid account | Often yes, with an opt-out | Provider staff under stated conditions | Same setting, do not assume paying changes it |
| Company workspace (business or enterprise style plan) | Generally no | Provider staff under stated conditions, plus your company's admins | Your company's agreement and admin settings |
| Tool built on the provider's developer interface | Generally no, per the provider's stated terms | Depends on how the builder stores logs | The builder's own privacy policy |

The row people misjudge most is the second one. Paying for a personal plan does not automatically move you into the business tier of protection. What matters is the type of account, not the price.

You can usually tell which situation you are in by asking three questions:

1. Did I sign up with my own email or a company email?
2. Does my company have a written policy on AI tools?
3. Did IT or my manager set this account up for me?

If the answers are "my own email," "no," and "no," assume you are on a personal account and act accordingly. If you cannot answer, ask. It is a normal question, and asking is better than guessing.

## A safe exercise: the placeholder test

**Try the placeholder test: rewrite one real work question with made-up names and numbers, then compare the answers. It takes ten minutes, costs nothing, and shows you that useful help rarely needs private details. Do it in a new chat with training switched off.**

The goal is not to fear the tool. It is to notice that you control what goes in.

### Steps

1. Open your data controls and switch off training use if it is on, or open a temporary chat.
2. Pick a real task, such as a hard email to a client about a late payment.
3. Write the version you would naturally type. Do not send it. Look at it and circle every real name, company, amount, and address.
4. Rewrite it with placeholders: "Client A," "Company B," "$X," "the March invoice."
5. Send the placeholder version and ask for a draft.
6. Swap your real details back in yourself, on your own machine.

Most people find the answer is just as useful. The model needs the shape of the problem, not the identities. This is the same skill behind clear requests in general, and the [Prompt Engineering for Beginners: A Plain-English Start](/articles/prompt-engineering-for-beginners) article shows how to describe a situation well without over-sharing.

### A quick checklist before you hit send

- Does this include a name, address, or account number?
- Would I be uncomfortable if a coworker read it over my shoulder?
- Is my company's policy silent, unclear, or against it?
- Could I describe the situation with placeholders instead?

If any answer worries you, edit first.

## When this advice will not protect you

**Privacy settings reduce risk but do not remove it. Accounts can be compromised, shared screens leak, legal orders can force providers to keep data, and policies change. Anything that would harm someone if it leaked, such as health records or passwords, should stay out of any chatbot.**

Placeholders and opt-outs are habits, not shields. A few honest limits:

- **Settings are not a legal guarantee.** A toggle describes current behavior. Your company's contract is what binds anyone.
- **Legal requests exist.** Providers can be required by courts to keep or hand over data in some circumstances, and that can override normal deletion practices.
- **Deleted is not always gone immediately.** Retention periods vary, and the details are in the provider's policy.
- **Your own rules may be stricter.** Some employers, schools, and regulated fields ban pasting client or patient information into any outside tool. Follow the stricter rule.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does ChatGPT share my questions with other people?

Not by default. Other users cannot browse your chats. The main exception is a share link you create yourself. Separately, the provider's authorized staff may review conversations in limited situations, and your employer may have access on a work account.

### Can OpenAI employees read my chats?

Under stated conditions, yes. Providers describe reasons such as abuse detection, security investigations, support requests, and legal obligations. It is not described as routine reading of everything, but it is also not zero. Read the current privacy policy for the exact wording.

### Does turning off chat history or training make me fully private?

No. It reduces how your conversations are used and stored, but it does not make them invisible to the provider or immune to account theft or legal requests. It is a useful step, not a guarantee.

### Is a company ChatGPT plan safer than my personal account?

Generally it offers stronger promises, such as no training on your data by default and admin controls. The trade-off is that your company's administrators may have visibility. Confirm the details with your IT team or in your company's agreement.

### What should I never type into a chatbot?

Passwords, payment card numbers, government ID numbers, medical or legal details about real people, and anything your employer has told you is confidential. When unsure, replace details with placeholders or leave them out.
