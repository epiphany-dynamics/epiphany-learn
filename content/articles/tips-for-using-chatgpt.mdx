**The tips that help most are not about wording. Start a fresh chat when the topic changes, check what the assistant has saved about you and clear what is wrong, and spend two minutes filling in the custom instructions box so you stop re-explaining your job every single session.**

## Start a new chat more often than feels natural

**One long thread feels tidy, but it drags every earlier turn along with it. The assistant re-reads the whole conversation each time, so old corrections, abandoned drafts, and tangents compete with your current question. When the topic changes, open a new chat and paste in only the part that still matters.**

Think of a chat window as a desk. Everything you set down stays there, including the memo you already threw out. Most beginners keep one thread open for weeks because starting over feels wasteful, then wonder why the answers get vague around day four.

Watch for these signals that the desk is too crowded: it reuses an instruction you withdrew two hours ago, it keeps citing a file you already replaced, its tone drifts back toward an earlier project, or replies start arriving slower and shorter. Clutter is one ordinary cause of confident nonsense, and the other causes are worth knowing too, which is why [Why Does ChatGPT Give Wrong Answers? A Plain-English Answer](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) is a useful companion to this habit.

Starting fresh costs you one paste of three sentences. That is all.

| Situation | Best move | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small edits to a draft it just wrote | Continue the thread | The draft is the context |
| New client, new project, new question | New chat | Nothing earlier applies |
| You corrected the same mistake twice and it came back | New chat | The old wording keeps winning |
| You uploaded a replacement version of a file | New chat | Both versions are now in play |
| Replies feel slower, shorter, or oddly generic | New chat | The thread has grown heavy |
| A personal question you do not want mixed with work context | New chat | Keeps the two separate |

## What the assistant is quietly remembering about you

**There are two kinds of memory. One lives inside a single chat and disappears when you close it. The other saves facts across all your chats: your job, your city, your preferences, project names you mentioned once. That second kind keeps working after you have forgotten telling it anything.**

Look in Settings, then in the section usually labeled Personalization or Data Controls. Menu names move between updates and differ by plan, so if the wording does not match, search the settings screen for the word memory rather than assuming the feature is missing.

Read the saved list. Beginners are often surprised by what is in there: an old job title, a client name from a project that ended, a stray preference from a bad day. Stale facts quietly steer future answers, which is how one client's details end up in another client's draft.

You have three controls. Delete individual saved items you no longer want. Turn the whole feature off if you would rather supply context yourself each time. Or use a temporary chat, which answers your question without saving anything. If you are weighing what belongs in the box at all, [Is ChatGPT Confidential? A Plain-English Answer](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) covers that question directly.

## The two-minute setup that stops you re-explaining your job

**Custom instructions are a short standing note the assistant reads before every new chat. Filling them in once means you stop opening each session with a paragraph about who you are and what you do. Keep it short and boring, because a long instruction block starts fighting your actual question.**

Find the same Settings area, look for Custom Instructions or Personalization, and fill five lines:

1. **My work:** office manager at a three-person physical therapy clinic.
2. **Who reads my writing:** patients and two front desk staff, not clinicians.
3. **Tone:** plain, warm, no jargon, no hype.
4. **Format:** short paragraphs, bullets only when comparing options.
5. **Always:** show your reasoning in one line, and tell me when you are unsure.

That last line does real work. It gives you a habit of asking for the reasoning rather than the conclusion, which is the same habit behind [ChatGPT Excel formulas: ask well, then check the answer](/articles/chatgpt-excel-formulas), where the formula matters far less than understanding what it is doing to your spreadsheet.

Rewrite these lines whenever your job changes. Six words of stale context is worse than none.

## A safe exercise you can finish this week

**Do this with invented details, not real client records, patient information, or anything covered by a confidentiality agreement. The point is to feel the difference between a cluttered thread and a clean one, and to see your own saved memories with your own eyes. Fifteen minutes, start to finish.**

1. Open Settings and read every saved memory. Delete anything stale or wrong.
2. Fill in the five custom instruction lines above using your real role but no names.
3. Open a brand new chat and ask a small work question with no setup at all.
4. Notice what it already knew. That is your setup doing the work.
5. Now ask an unrelated question in the same thread, then a third. Watch whether the answers start blending.
6. Start a fresh chat for that third question and compare the two answers side by side.

Most people find the fresh answer tighter and more on target. If yours are identical, that is a useful result too, and it means your threads are not yet long enough to matter.

## When these habits are the wrong move

**Fresh chats have a real cost. You retype context, you lose the thread of a decision, and on a long multi-day project a single well-tended conversation often beats ten disconnected ones. Turning memory off means explaining yourself again every morning. Neither habit is free, so choose per task.**

Keep one thread when you are iterating on a single document and each version builds on the last. Keep memory on if you use the assistant daily for the same job and the saved facts are accurate. Start fresh and switch memory off when the work is sensitive, occasional, or unrelated to everything else you do.

Also worth saying plainly: settings menus change, features roll out to different plans at different times, and what you see may not match this article word for word. Trust the screen in front of you over any guide, including this one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does starting a new chat make ChatGPT forget everything?

It forgets the conversation, not your settings. Custom instructions and saved memories carry across into every new chat automatically. What disappears is the specific back-and-forth, the drafts, and the corrections from that thread, which is usually exactly what you wanted to leave behind.

### How do I see what ChatGPT has saved about me?

Open Settings and look for Personalization or Data Controls, then find the memory section and open the saved list. You can read each item and delete them individually. Exact menu labels shift between updates and plans, so search the settings screen for memory if the wording differs.

### Do custom instructions apply to conversations I already started?

Usually they take effect in new chats rather than retroactively reshaping old ones. If you have just written your instructions and want to see them working, open a fresh chat and ask something small. Judging them from inside an old thread will give you a muddy result.

### Is a long thread ever better than a fresh one?

Yes. When you are refining one document across several sittings, the history is the context and starting over throws it away. The rule is about topic changes, not chat length. A focused thread about one deliverable can run a long time and stay sharp.

### Will turning memory off make the answers worse?

It makes them less personalized, not less accurate. You simply supply the context yourself at the top of each chat, which many people prefer because they can see exactly what the assistant is working from. For sensitive or one-off questions, that visibility is worth the extra sentence.

Pick one of these three habits and use it for a week before adding the next. Clearing stale memories is the fastest win, and it takes about as long as reading this paragraph.
