**ChatGPT can help an accountant draft, explain, reorganize, and summarize. It cannot be trusted to produce a number you have not checked yourself. Anonymized transaction descriptions and general policy questions are reasonable inputs. Client names, figures tied to an identifiable person, and anything from a return are not. Verification sits after every output.**

Most writing about AI in accounting argues about whether the job survives. This article assumes you still have the job and a to-do list, and answers a smaller question: what does an assistant actually do for you between 9am and lunch on an ordinary Tuesday, and what has to happen before any of it leaves your desk.

## What a bookkeeper actually uses it for on a Tuesday

**The useful work is language work, not arithmetic. Rewriting messy vendor descriptions, drafting the third receipt-chasing email of the week, explaining a treatment in plain English for a client, turning close notes into a checklist. In each case the assistant produces words you review, not figures you file.**

Here is what a realistic morning looks like. You have a list of forty vendor descriptions pulled from a bank feed, full of abbreviations and payment processor noise. You paste them with the client name stripped out and ask for a suggested category for each, plus a flag on anything ambiguous. You get a first pass in seconds and spend your time on the eight it flagged rather than all forty.

Then a client emails asking why their software subscription was split across two months. You know the answer. You do not want to spend twenty minutes phrasing it kindly. You describe the situation generically and ask for a short, warm explanation at a sixth-grade reading level, then edit it into your own voice and add the actual dates yourself.

Later you need a spreadsheet formula you half remember. That is a genuinely good use, and it has its own method: our guide to [asking well and then checking the answer on ChatGPT Excel formulas](/articles/chatgpt-excel-formulas) walks through describing your columns properly and testing the result on rows where you already know the answer.

What none of that includes is letting it reconcile an account, compute a depreciation schedule you then trust, or decide a treatment. Those stay with you.

## What can go in the chat box and what cannot

**The dividing line is identifiability, not sensitivity in the abstract. A transaction description with no name attached identifies nobody. The same line labeled with a client and a date does. Returns, payroll data, and anything with a taxpayer number stay out entirely, regardless of settings.**

| What you were about to paste | Send it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Monthly software subscription, $49, recurring" with no client attached | Yes | Generic enough that it points to no one |
| "How is a prepaid annual insurance premium normally expensed?" | Yes | A general knowledge question with no client data in it |
| A draft client email with names, dates, and amounts removed | Yes | You reinsert the specifics after editing |
| "Reconcile these 40 lines from the Miller Dental March statement" | No | Names a client and ties real figures to them |
| Any page of a filed or draft return | No | The highest-sensitivity document on your desk |
| A payroll register with employee names | No | Third-party personal data you were trusted with, not yours to share |
| A client's accounting export uploaded as a file | Only with written client permission | Uploading is still sending |

That last row is where people slip, because attaching a file feels different from typing. It is not. If you do work with files, read [the calm first-time walkthrough of ChatGPT file upload](/articles/chatgpt-file-upload) before you attach anything, and understand what happens to a document after you send it. The broader question of who can see your chats is covered in our plain-English answer to [whether ChatGPT is confidential](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential). Short version: assume a chat is not a private workpaper, and check whether your engagement letters and your firm's data policy already say something about third-party tools.

## The verification step that sits after every output

**Nothing the assistant produces is finished. Treat every response as a first draft from a bright temp who has never seen your client file and will never tell you when they are guessing. Five checks, in order, every time, before anything is signed, sent, or booked.**

1. **Read it as a draft, not an answer.** Ask yourself what you would say if a junior handed you this exact paragraph.
2. **Recompute every number by hand or in your software.** If it produced a total, a percentage, or a proration, you calculate it independently. If you cannot be bothered to check it, you cannot use it.
3. **Verify any rule, threshold, or citation at the source.** Look it up in the actual guidance or publication. A plausible-sounding rule with a plausible-sounding reference is the easiest thing in the world for a language model to produce.
4. **Ask what it assumed.** Prompt it directly: "list the assumptions you made." Half the time the wrongness lives in an assumption it never mentioned, like a calendar year end or a cash basis.
5. **Sign only what you can defend without mentioning the tool.** If you could not explain the reasoning to a reviewer in your own words, it does not go out.

Checks two and three are the ones people quietly drop when they are busy. Those are the two that keep you out of trouble.

## Why a confident wrong number is the specific danger here

**In most jobs a wrong AI answer looks obviously off and gets caught. In accounting it looks exactly like a right one. A number carries no visible uncertainty, arrives in the same calm tone as everything else, and lands in a workflow built on the assumption that figures have been checked.**

A language model predicts likely text. When a figure is the likely-looking next thing, it produces a figure. It does not know whether it computed it or invented something that resembles a computation, and its tone is identical either way. There is no wobble in the voice, no hedge, no asterisk. Our explanation of [why ChatGPT gives wrong answers](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) covers the mechanism in plain language, and it is worth ten minutes if you have never read one.

The practical consequence is that the danger scales with how mundane the task feels. A wrong strategic opinion gets debated. A wrong reconciliation gets booked, closed, and discovered in November. That asymmetry is also the honest reason the profession is not simply being handed over to software, which we broke down task by task in our look at [whether AI is replacing accountants](/articles/ai-replacing-accountants).

## A safe exercise to try this week

**Spend fifteen minutes on data that cannot hurt anyone. Invent a small vendor list, ask for categories, then grade the output. You will learn more about where this tool is reliable from one deliberate test than from a month of cautious avoidance.**

Make up ten transaction lines yourself. Something like "SQ *BLUE ROOF CAFE 14.20", "ANNUAL DOMAIN RENEWAL 22.00", "TRANSFER TO SAVINGS 500.00", "STATE FILING FEE 300.00". No real client, no real amounts.

Then ask: "Here are ten transaction descriptions from a small service business. Suggest a likely expense category for each, mark any you are unsure about, and explain the two you are least confident on."

Now grade it. Where did it do well? Where did it categorize confidently and wrongly? Did the transfer get treated as an expense? Did it flag the ambiguous ones or breeze past them? That last question tells you the most, because an assistant that never signals uncertainty is one you have to check line by line forever.

## When this is the wrong tool entirely

**Skip it for anything where the answer must be defensible to a regulator, a lender, or a court, and for any client who has not agreed to third-party tools. Skip it for judgment calls with a materiality threshold attached. Skip it when checking the output would take longer than doing the work.**

That third one is the honest limitation nobody advertises. For a task you have done two hundred times, generating and then properly verifying a response can be slower than simply doing it. The assistant earns its place on unfamiliar phrasing, first drafts, explanations, and structure. It does not earn its place on work your hands already know.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I paste a client's bank statement into ChatGPT?

Not without explicit written permission from that client, and even then, think hard. A statement ties real amounts to a named person or business. If you need help with categorization logic, invent representative lines instead and apply the logic yourself.

### Will it do my bookkeeping for me?

No. It can suggest, draft, explain, and reformat. Entry, reconciliation, and anything you sign remain yours, because the tool has no access to the ledger and no way to tell you when it has guessed.

### Is the paid version safer than the free one?

Paid tiers usually offer more control over whether conversations are used for training, and business tiers add administrative settings. That reduces one risk. It does not make client-identifying data appropriate to send, and it does nothing about accuracy.

### What if I already pasted something I should not have?

Delete the conversation, check your data controls, and if the information belonged to a client, tell your supervisor or follow your firm's incident process. It is a much smaller problem when reported the same day.

### Can it help me prepare a tax return?

It can help you understand a concept in general terms so you can research it properly. It should not touch return data, and any rule it states needs verification against current guidance before you rely on it.

If you take one thing from this: build the verification step into the habit now, while the stakes are a made-up vendor list, rather than discovering you skipped it during a busy season close.
