**AI can safely take the first draft of repetitive nonprofit writing: grant report drafts built from your own notes, donor thank-you variants, and one program update rewritten for three audiences. Keep it away from two things without a human check: anything naming a real beneficiary, and anything a funder will read as fact.**

You do not need an IT department to try this, and you do not need to answer your board with a strategy deck. You need a short list of jobs an assistant can help with, a shorter list of jobs it must never touch alone, and one line about donor data that never moves.

## What AI can actually take off your week

**Start with the writing you already do from information you already have. A grant report first draft from your own notes, five thank-you variants for a mail merge, a program update rewritten for a board packet, a newsletter, and a partner email. These are rephrasing jobs, not research jobs, and that distinction is the whole safety story.**

If the information already lives in your files and your head, and the job is to shape it into words, an assistant is genuinely useful. If the job is to *know* something, it is not.

| Weekly job | Safe to hand off as a first draft | What you still do yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Grant report narrative | Yes, from your notes and last year's report | Verify every number, outcome, and date |
| Donor thank-you variants | Yes, several versions of one template | Approve tone, merge names, sign the big ones |
| One update, three audiences | Yes | Confirm nothing was invented in the reshaping |
| Volunteer role descriptions | Yes | Check hours, requirements, supervisor name |
| Meeting notes into action items | Yes, if the notes are yours | Correct who agreed to what |
| A beneficiary story for an appeal | No | Write it with the person, with consent |
| A new statistic about your field | No | Find and cite the real source |

The useful rows share one trait: you are the source and the assistant is the typist.

### Why first drafts change the math

A first draft is not the time saver people advertise. The saver is smaller and more honest: it removes the blank page. Most program directors lose more time deciding how to start a report than writing it. Handing over the opening structure, then rewriting it in your own voice, is a real half hour back without handing over judgment.

## The two places AI must not go without a human

**Two lines do not move. First, anything about a named beneficiary: a client story, a case note, an intake detail. Second, anything a funder will read as fact: numbers served, outcomes, dates, spending. A person who knows the truth signs off on both before they leave your building.**

### Named beneficiaries

The people you serve did not consent to being processed by a vendor's system. That holds even when the story is flattering, even when the name is changed, and even when you plan to delete the chat afterward. If you want a story for an appeal, get it from the person, in their words, with written permission, and write it yourself. An assistant can help you tighten a story you already have consent for. It cannot be the place the story first appears.

### Anything a funder reads as fact

Assistants produce confident sentences whether or not the underlying detail is right, which is why a plausible wrong number is the dangerous failure mode, not an obvious one. The clear explanation in [Why Does ChatGPT Give Wrong Answers?](/articles/why-does-chatgpt-give-wrong-answers) is worth twenty minutes before your first grant report draft. In practice: if a sentence contains a number, a date, a percentage, or a claim about results, you check it against your own records before it moves.

## The donor data line, said plainly

**Donor records are the most sensitive file your organization holds, and pasting them into a chat assistant is a decision, not a shortcut. The plain line: no donor names, no giving amounts tied to a person, no addresses, no email lists. Describe the pattern instead and let your mail merge attach the names.**

You almost never need the actual data. Instead of pasting a spreadsheet, say: "Write four thank-you variants for donors who gave between one hundred and five hundred dollars to a youth literacy program, warm but not effusive, four sentences each." You get the same output with none of the exposure.

The reason to hold this line is that consumer chat tools are not built as record systems, and their privacy settings vary by plan and change over time. [Is ChatGPT Confidential?](/articles/is-chatgpt-confidential) explains what actually happens to what you type, in plain language, and it is the piece to forward to whoever manages your database.

## Try this in twenty minutes: one update, three audiences

**Take one program update you already wrote and ask for three versions: one for the board, one for the newsletter, one for a funder check-in. Use no names and no numbers you have not verified. Twenty minutes, nothing at risk, and you will see exactly where the assistant helps and where it drifts.**

1. Copy a program update you wrote in the last month. Delete every proper name and replace specific figures with placeholders like `[NUMBER SERVED]`.
2. Paste it and ask for three rewrites, naming the audience and the length for each.
3. Read the board version first. Ask yourself one question: did it add anything I did not say?
4. Rewrite the best version in your own voice. It will need it.
5. Put the real numbers back in by hand, checking each one against your records.

Step three is the skill. Everything else is typing. If the exercise goes well and you want a structured path rather than one-off experiments, the [Epiphany Learn modules](/modules/) start from zero and assume no technical background.

## What to tell your board

**Your board asked a governance question, not a technology question. Answer it in three parts: here are the three tasks we are testing, here are the two categories that never touch AI without a human, and here is who reviews before anything goes out. Put that on one page and revisit it in ninety days.**

That one page is worth more than a tool subscription. It shows the board you took the question seriously without committing staff time to a project nobody scoped. If someone asks about disclosure, the practical norms in [AI Etiquette: The Unwritten Rules, Answered Plainly](/articles/ai-etiquette) cover when to say a draft was AI assisted and when nobody expects you to.

## When not to bring AI in at all

**Skip AI when the real problem is a broken process, when the writing carries grief or crisis, and when your board wants a headcount cut. A slow grant cycle caused by missing intake data does not get faster with better prose. Fix the intake, then bring in the drafting help.**

This is the most common expensive mistake. If your reports are late because three staff track outcomes in three different spreadsheets, an assistant makes late reports arrive slightly more fluently. The honest first question is whether you need a rule based fix instead, which is exactly what [Do you actually need AI?](/modules/module-5/ai-or-automation) walks through. Condolence letters, crisis communications, and anything a family will read on the worst day of their year stay fully human. Not because a tool would fail at the grammar, but because the point of those letters is that a person sat down and wrote them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can we do this on a free plan?

Yes for the exercise above. Free tiers handle rewriting and drafting fine. The differences show up in file uploads, longer documents, and privacy controls, which matter more once you move past testing.

### Will AI write our grant application for us?

It will draft sections you can edit. It will not know your outcomes, your budget, or the funder's unwritten preferences. Treat it as help with the narrative sections and never with the numbers.

### How do we know if it made something up?

Check anything checkable against your own records, and be most suspicious of the specific and confident sentences. If a claim appeared without you supplying it, assume it needs a source before it ships.

### Do we have to tell donors we used AI?

There is no universal rule. The reasonable standard: nobody expects disclosure on a formatted thank-you, and people do expect a personal note to be personal. Decide it once, write it down, and be consistent.

### Where should a total beginner start?

Pick the single most repetitive writing job on your desk and run the three audiences exercise on it this week. If you want the fundamentals first, the [beginner articles](/articles/) cover prompting, uploads, and privacy without jargon.
