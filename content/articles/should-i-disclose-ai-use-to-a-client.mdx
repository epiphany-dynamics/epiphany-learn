**Yes, in most client relationships you should disclose meaningful AI use, especially for judgment calls, and always avoid putting client data into a tool the client hasn't approved. The real risk isn't admitting you used AI. It's what you typed into the tool before you disclosed anything.**

## What "disclosing AI use" actually means

**Disclosure means telling a client, in plain terms, that AI helped produce part of their deliverable and that you reviewed the result. It does not mean confessing every keystroke or listing every tool you touched during the project.**

Most clients don't need a technical breakdown. They need to know whether the judgment behind their work is yours or a machine's, and whether their information stayed safe while you worked.

Think of it like disclosing that you used a template or a research assistant. Nobody expects a lawyer to explain every database search behind a contract. But if a paralegal drafted the whole thing unsupervised, the client would want to know. AI sits somewhere in that same range, depending on how much of the thinking it did.

## The real question is the data boundary, not the disclosure

**Before you decide what to say, decide what you're allowed to type. The disclosure conversation matters less than the boundary you set before you ever open a chat window, because once client data leaves your hands and enters a public AI tool, you usually can't undo that.**

This is the part that gets skipped in most "should I tell my client" debates.

Ask yourself two separate questions:

1. Am I putting anything identifiable about this client into the tool: names, financials, contracts, health information, internal strategy?
2. Would this client be upset if they saw exactly what I typed?

If the answer to either is yes, the fix isn't a disclosure statement. It's removing the identifying details before you type anything, or not using that tool for this task at all. Our article on [what not to share with ChatGPT at work](/articles/what-not-to-share-with-chatgpt-at-work) walks through the specific categories of information that shouldn't go into a general-purpose AI tool, and the same list applies directly to client work, often with higher stakes because the data isn't yours.

## When you should tell a client, and when you don't need to

**Tell clients when AI shaped a decision, a recommendation, or a substantial share of the writing they're paying for. You don't usually need to mention AI for small mechanical help like fixing grammar, reformatting a spreadsheet, or brainstorming options you then rewrote yourself.**

The dividing line is judgment, not tool use.

Here's a simple way to sort common scenarios:

| Task | Disclose to client? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| AI drafted the client's strategy memo, you edited lightly | Yes | The core judgment came from the tool |
| AI helped you brainstorm five options, you chose and wrote the final one | Optional, but transparency builds trust | You made the call; AI was a starting point |
| AI checked a spreadsheet formula for errors | No | Mechanical check, not client-facing judgment |
| AI summarized a client's uploaded document to save you reading time | Yes, and confirm the upload was permitted | Their data touched a third-party tool |
| AI wrote the first draft of client-facing copy you fully rewrote | Optional, disclose if your contract requires it | Final judgment and voice are yours |

## How to disclose without sounding defensive

**Say what you did in one plain sentence, name the safeguard you used, and move on. Over-explaining or apologizing signals that you think AI use was wrong, which makes clients more suspicious, not less. A short, confident statement works better than a long justification.**

A workable script:

"I used an AI tool to help draft the first version of this section and to check the numbers, then reviewed and edited everything myself before sending it to you. No client-identifying details went into the tool."

That's it. It states the use, states the check, and states the data boundary. If a client asks follow-up questions, answer them honestly. Most won't ask, because the statement already answered the two things they actually care about.

## A safe exercise: check your own risk before your next client deliverable

**Before your next AI-assisted client task, run through a five-minute check that separates what's safe to type from what isn't, and decide your disclosure line in advance rather than improvising it afterward. Doing this once turns it into a habit instead of a decision you make under deadline pressure.**

1. Write down the client task you're about to do with AI help.
2. List every piece of client-specific information the task involves (names, numbers, documents, strategy).
3. Cross out anything from that list you would need to type into the AI tool to get useful help. If the list isn't empty, replace it with placeholders or generic descriptions before you type.
4. Decide, before you start, whether this task counts as "judgment" or "mechanical" using the table above.
5. If it's judgment, draft your one-sentence disclosure now, before you send the final deliverable.

## One limitation worth knowing

**This approach is a reasonable default, not a legal guarantee. Some contracts, industries, and jurisdictions have specific AI disclosure or data-handling rules that override general guidance like this, so check your client agreement and any applicable regulations before assuming a one-sentence disclosure is enough.**

When in doubt, ask the client directly what they'd want, rather than guessing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I legally have to tell a client I used AI?

In most freelance and small-business relationships, there's no blanket law requiring disclosure, but some contracts, client policies, or regulated industries (legal, healthcare, financial services) do require it. Check your specific agreement rather than assuming general guidance covers your case.

### What if my client would be upset even about mechanical AI use?

If a client has explicitly said no AI tools at all, respect that regardless of how minor the use feels to you. When in doubt, ask before the project starts rather than disclosing after the fact.

### Can I use AI on a client project without ever telling them?

You can for low-stakes mechanical help, but silence becomes a problem the moment a client finds out later and feels misled. A short disclosure up front is almost always safer than an accidental discovery down the line.

### Does disclosing AI use make me look less skilled?

Usually not, if you frame it around judgment: you chose what to use it for, checked the output, and take responsibility for the final result. Clients generally worry more about unchecked mistakes than about tool use itself.

### What's the safest first step if I'm not sure how to handle this?

Start with the data boundary, not the wording. Decide what client information can never go into an AI tool, and build your disclosure habit around that line. Our beginner articles and learning modules both have practical starting points if you want a structured way to build this habit.
