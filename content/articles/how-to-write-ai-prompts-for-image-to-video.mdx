**To write an image-to-video prompt, describe the motion you want, not what the picture already shows. Runway's guidance says the starting image sets composition, subject, lighting and style, so your text should cover movement. Begin with one simple sentence, watch the result, then add detail. Expect some trial and error, because outcomes are not guaranteed.**

If you have a still photo and want it to move, the hardest part is knowing what to type. Most beginners describe the photo again. That feels natural, but it wastes the prompt. This guide follows Runway's public image-to-video prompting guide and keeps to what that guide says. It does not cover making a new image from text, and it does not promise any particular result.

## What an image-to-video prompt does

**An image-to-video prompt tells the tool how the scene should move. The image you upload already supplies the composition, subject matter, lighting and style, according to Runway's help guide. Your words add the missing piece: what moves, how fast or gently, and whether the camera moves too.**

Runway describes image-to-video models as tools that transform an image into a video with a text prompt. The image guides the video. That means the picture is your first instruction, and the prompt is the second one.

Think of it as handing a photographer's assistant a print and saying, "Now make it move." They can see the print. They need to hear about the movement.

### What the tool can already see

- Who or what is in the frame
- Where things sit relative to each other
- The light and the overall look

### What only you can say

- Which part of the scene should move
- Whether the movement is slow, steady or sudden
- Whether the viewpoint should shift

## Describe motion, not the picture

**Skip the nouns and colors the image already shows. Runway's guidance says effective prompts focus almost exclusively on motion, so write verbs and movement: leaves sway, steam rises, the camera drifts forward. If a word would describe a still photo, it probably belongs elsewhere or nowhere.**

Here is the difference using an invented example. Say your photo shows a green kettle on a wooden counter beside a window.

| Prompt style | Example | Why it helps or not |
|---|---|---|
| Repeats the image | "A green kettle on a wooden counter by a window" | The tool already sees this. No motion is described. |
| Describes motion | "Steam rises slowly from the spout. The curtain moves gently." | Names what moves and how. |
| Adds camera intent | "Steam rises slowly. The camera moves slightly closer." | Adds a viewpoint request. It may or may not be followed. |

The second row is the pattern to copy. Pick the one or two things that matter most and give each a verb and a pace.

### Words that carry motion

Use plain verbs: drift, sway, ripple, rise, turn, fall, glide. Add a pace word such as slowly, steadily or gently. You do not need technical film terms to start.

## A safe first exercise

**Use a photo of an ordinary object you own, such as a coffee mug by a window, with nothing private in frame. Write one plain sentence about motion, run it, and note what changed. The goal is learning how wording affects movement, not producing something polished.**

Choose a photo with no faces, no documents, no screens and no client material. A mug, a plant or a view from your porch all work. If you want a reminder of why private material stays out, the guide on Google Lens identify plant photo shows the same careful habit with ordinary photos.

Follow these steps:

1. Pick one photo and write down the one thing you want to move.
2. Write one sentence with a verb and a pace, such as "Steam rises slowly from the mug."
3. Run it once and watch the whole clip.
4. Write one line: what moved the way you wanted, and what did not.

Do not judge the first try harshly. You are collecting information about how your words relate to the result.

## Refine one change at a time

**Runway recommends starting with a simple prompt about the most critical motion, then adding detail as needed. Change one thing per attempt so you can tell which words caused which difference. Think of it as editing a short motion specification, not rewriting from scratch each time.**

A motion specification is just a short list of what should move and how. You build it up in layers. Here is an illustrative path, not a record of a real test:

| Attempt | Prompt | What you changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Steam rises." | Started simple |
| 2 | "Steam rises slowly." | Added pace |
| 3 | "Steam rises slowly. The curtain moves gently." | Added a second motion |
| 4 | "Steam rises slowly. The curtain moves gently. The camera holds still." | Added a camera note |

If attempt 3 looks wrong, you know the curtain line is the likely cause. If you had changed three things at once, you would not know.

### A quick checklist before each run

- Does every phrase describe movement?
- Did I change only one thing since last time?
- Is the most important motion stated first?
- Have I kept the prompt short enough to read at a glance?

## When not to rely on this

**Do not treat any prompt as a guarantee. Results vary, so a camera move you ask for may not appear, and nothing here promises that people or objects stay recognizable. Skip uploading private or confidential images, and check you have rights to any image you use.**

Runway's guide describes how to write prompts. It does not say a given sentence will always produce a given movement. Treat each run as a draft. Some attempts will ignore part of what you wrote.

Also remember that this advice covers an image you already have. It does not teach you how to make the starting image. Plan limits and pricing change, so check Runway's own pages for current details rather than relying on this article.

If a clip is going to a client or the public, review every second of it yourself before use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should an image-to-video prompt describe motion rather than repeat the starting picture?

Name what moves and how, using verbs and a pace. Runway's guidance says the image already defines composition, subject, lighting and style. A prompt like "Steam rises slowly" adds new information. A prompt like "a green kettle on a counter" repeats what the tool can already see.

### How long should the prompt be?

Start short. Runway recommends beginning with a simple prompt focused on the most critical motion, then adding detail as needed. One or two sentences is a reasonable first attempt. Add a line only when you can say what the new line is meant to change.

### Can I ask for a specific camera move?

You can describe one, and it may help. There is no guarantee the tool will follow it exactly. Treat camera requests like any other line: add one at a time, watch the result, and keep or remove it based on what you see.

### Does the video keep the people in my photo looking the same?

This article cannot promise that. Runway's guidance says the image guides the video, but it does not guarantee identity is preserved. Use a photo without people for practice, and check the output closely before using any clip that includes someone.

### What should I practice on first?

Use a harmless photo of an object you own, with nothing private in it. A mug, a plant or a window view works. Keep the first prompt to one motion sentence, then change one thing per attempt.

Practice on low stakes tasks first, then move to real work.
