**A good AI photo edit prompt names three things: the photo you supply, the one change you want, and the elements that must stay the same. Then you compare the original and the result side by side with a person. You get examples, a safe practice exercise, and a checklist for judging the edit.**

## What a photo editing prompt needs

**Name the photo, the single change, and everything that must stay the same. Google's documentation for image generation describes editing an image you provide with text prompts, and shows a pattern that says to change only one element and keep the rest the same. Treat the preserve line as a request, not a guarantee.**

Most beginners write only the change: "make the sky more dramatic." That leaves the tool free to decide what else to adjust. A better prompt has three parts.

1. **The supplied photo.** Say which image you are working from, in words. "The photo I uploaded of a kitchen counter" is enough.
2. **The requested change.** One change, described plainly.
3. **The preserved elements.** A short list of what must not move: faces, text, colors, lighting, layout.

Google's documentation gives a template for this. In its words: "Using the provided image, change only the [specific element] to [new element/description]. Keep everything else in the image exactly the same, preserving the original style, lighting, and composition." The same page also describes defining a "mask" in conversation, which means telling the tool to edit one part of an image while leaving the rest untouched.

Those are documented features. They are not promises about any single result. Your output may still shift things you asked it to keep, so you check it yourself.

### Why one change at a time

If you ask for five changes and the result looks off, you cannot tell which request caused the problem. One change gives you one thing to inspect. It is the same habit that makes the lesson on your first real conversation with AI useful: small asks, then read the answer carefully.

## Three prompt examples you can adapt

**Each example below follows one pattern: it identifies the photo, states one change, and lists what to keep. The photos are hypothetical. Swap in your own details, and keep the change small, because a prompt with one clear change is easier to check than a prompt with five.**

### Example 1: a product photo

> Using the provided photo of a ceramic mug on a wooden table, change only the mug color from white to dark blue. Keep the table, the window light, the shadow under the mug, the camera angle, and the crop exactly the same.

### Example 2: a family snapshot

> Using the provided photo of my family at a picnic, remove only the red cooler at the left edge. Keep every person's face, clothing, and pose unchanged. Keep the grass, the sky, and the overall lighting the same.

### Example 3: a room photo

> Using the provided photo of my living room, change only the wall color from beige to light gray. Keep the furniture, the rug, the picture frames, the window, and the lighting as they are.

Notice what the prompts leave out. They do not say "make it better" or "fix everything." Vague goals invite changes you did not ask for.

These examples edit an image that already exists. If you later want to describe movement instead of a still change, the article on [how to write AI prompts for image to video](/articles/how-to-write-ai-prompts-for-image-to-video) covers that separate skill.

## A safe practice exercise

**Use a photo of an everyday object you own, such as a coffee mug on a wooden table, with no people, no address, and nothing private visible. Ask for one small change, such as a blue mug instead of a white one, and review the result with someone beside you.**

Follow these steps. Plan on about 20 minutes your first time.

1. **Pick the photo.** Choose a plain object photo. Do not use a child's photo, a document, a screen showing messages, or anything with a visible address.
2. **Write down your three parts.** Photo: the mug on the table. Change: the mug becomes dark blue. Preserve: table, light, shadow, angle, crop.
3. **Run the prompt once.** Do not keep re-rolling until something looks good. Judge the first result honestly.
4. **Save both images.** Keep the original and the edited version under clear names, such as "mug-original" and "mug-edited."
5. **Compare with a person.** Follow the next section.

If the edit goes wrong, nothing is lost. That is the point of a low stakes first try. The lesson on AI for everyday tasks uses the same idea of practicing on harmless material first.

## How to run the before-and-after comparison

**Put the original and the edited photo side by side and ask a second person to name every difference they see, before you tell them what you asked for. Then score each preserved element. A short written record beats memory, because small drifts are easy to miss.**

The order matters. If you announce the requested change first, your helper will look only for that change. Asking "what is different?" first shows you the changes nobody requested.

A few reading tips:

- Zoom in on edges, hands, text, and reflections. These are common places for small surprises, though I have not tested how often, so treat this as a checking habit rather than a rule.
- Check the area around the change as well as the change itself.
- Decide in advance what counts as a pass. For a mug photo, a slightly different shadow may be fine. For a family photo, a changed face is not.

The same attitude applies when an AI tool gives you an answer you cannot verify. The article on [treating a Google Lens plant result as a lead](/articles/google-lens-identify-plant-photo) makes the point that output is something you confirm, not something you accept.

## When not to use AI to edit a photo

**Skip AI editing when the photo is evidence, a legal or medical record, an ID, or an image of someone who has not agreed to it. Also skip it when accuracy matters more than looks. Preservation instructions can fail without any warning, so a clean-looking result proves nothing.**

Two further cautions:

- **Privacy.** Do not upload photos that contain private or confidential information to a tool you have not read the data terms for. When in doubt, use a different photo.
- **Honesty.** If an edited image will be shared with others, consider saying it was edited. A change that looks small to you can mislead a viewer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I describe a photo edit while stating what must stay unchanged?

Write one sentence for the change and one for the preserved elements. For example: "Change only the wall color to light gray. Keep the furniture, window, and lighting the same." Then check the result yourself, because a preserve instruction is a request the tool may not fully follow.

### Do I need special wording or technical terms?

No. Plain sentences work. Name the photo, say what to change, and list what to keep. Technical words like "mask" are optional. You can simply say "edit only this part and leave the rest alone."

### Will the AI always keep the parts I asked it to preserve?

Not necessarily. Preservation is an instruction, not a quality guarantee. That is why the side-by-side comparison matters. Review faces, text, colors, and edges before you use the image for anything that counts.

### What photo should I practice with first?

Use a simple object photo with no people and no private details, such as a mug, a plant pot, or a shelf. If the result is poor, you lose nothing and you learn what to watch for next time.
